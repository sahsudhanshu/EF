   Weighted Analysis of the 2024 Global Findex and an Institutional Payment-Safety

Abstract

This study examines how financial inclusion, digital payment use and reported cybersecurity
exposure relate to one another in India, and what this implies for entrepreneurs who serve first-
time digital users. We analyse the 2024 India Global Findex survey of 3,000 adults using survey
weights and an exploratory logistic model, compare India with peer economies, review 35
academic papers and several institutional sources, and code 5,758 public reviews of five payment
apps. Account ownership was 89.0%, but only 48.5% made or received a digital payment, and
57.7% of phone owners reported a device lock. Unsolicited money requests were reported by
13.1% of payment-using phone owners and 4.3% of non-users (adjusted odds ratio 3.45). App
reviews rarely described third-party fraud; complaints centred on unpaid rewards, failed
payments and weak support. An illustrative financial model for a bank-sold safety service breaks
even at eight paying institutions under stated assumptions. The data cannot show causation or
verified losses, and the commercial assumptions need pilot validation.

Keywords: financial inclusion; mobile banking; digital payments; cybersecurity; fraud exposure;
Global Findex; entrepreneurial finance; India

1. Introduction

1.1 Background

Access to formal finance is widely linked with household resilience, enterprise formation and
poverty reduction [1, 2]. Over the past decade, mobile phones and digital payment networks have
changed how that access is delivered, allowing people to pay, save and transfer money without
visiting a branch [3, 4]. India is a prominent example: its public digital identity and payment
infrastructure have been studied for effects on service delivery and household consumption [5,
6]. For a small business owner or a first-time digital customer, a bank account is useful only if it
can be used reliably to receive sales proceeds, pay suppliers and manage cash, and only if it can
be used without undue risk.

Three terms are used throughout. Financial inclusion is the use of formal financial services.
Account ownership measures access, whereas digital payment use measures activity. Reported
cybersecurity exposure, in this study, means a person's report of an unsolicited call or message
asking for money; it is a measure of exposure to a possible scam attempt, not verified fraud. The

                                                          1
World Bank's Global Findex is the most widely used cross-country source for the first two
measures [7, 8, 9].

1.2 Problem statement and importance

Account ownership, active digital use and safe digital use are different dimensions of inclusion.
A population that holds accounts but uses them little, or uses them without basic safeguards, can
be left exposed even as headline inclusion rises. Evidence from fraud and phishing research
shows that deception exploits ordinary behaviour rather than ignorance alone [10, 11], so the risk
grows as new, less experienced users enter digital channels. For entrepreneurs and financial
institutions the issue is practical: perceived credibility and trust influence whether customers
adopt mobile banking [12], and digital finance brings benefits alongside unresolved issues [13].

1.3 Research gap

The existing literature is strong on the economic benefits of mobile money and on the
behavioural causes of phishing, but the two strands are rarely joined in a single, recent,
nationally broad survey of India. Studies of digital inclusion seldom report device-level
safeguards or reported scam exposure, and security research seldom reports representative
adoption patterns by gender, age and income. Little work also translates such respondent-level
patterns into an entrepreneurial finance question: what service could be built, who would pay for
it, and what financial conditions would make it viable. This paper addresses these gaps with the
2024 India Global Findex microdata and a transparent, assumption-labelled venture model.

1.4 Research questions

RQ1. How do account ownership, digital payment use and device protection differ across
women and men, age groups and income quintiles in India, and how does India compare with
peer economies?
RQ2. Are digital payment users more likely than non-users to report unsolicited money requests,
after adjusting for demographic and economic characteristics?
RQ3. Among people who report an unsolicited money request, is having a device lock associated
with a lower share reporting that they sent money?
RQ4. Which groups show the largest gap between digital payment use and device protection?
RQ5. What do public app reviews and institutional documents reveal about fraud-related
grievances, security friction and support experiences?
RQ6. What financial conditions (price, costs and customer numbers) would allow an institution-
funded payment-safety service to break even, and how sensitive is that result to its assumptions?

2. Literature review

This review covers the economics of financial inclusion and mobile money, technology adoption
and trust in mobile banking, security behaviour and fraud, the economics of cyber risk, and the

                                                          2
entrepreneurial and policy literature on digital financial services. It follows the history of the
field, compares findings across sources and ends by identifying the gap this study fills. It draws
on 35 academic and policy sources; institutional documents used for context are cited in Sections
3 to 5.

2.1 Financial inclusion, development and measurement

Cross-country work established that deeper finance disproportionately raises the incomes of the
poorest and reduces inequality [14], and later reviews found that account access supports
inclusive growth, although the effects depend on how accounts are used [1]. Measurement
advanced with the first Global Findex in 2011 [7] and its repeated rounds [8, 9, 15], which
separated account ownership from usage. Index-based studies had earlier ranked countries on
banking penetration, availability and usage and associated inclusion with income, literacy,
urbanisation and infrastructure [16]. Financial illiteracy is widespread across the countries
studied [17]. Taken together, these sources justify treating ownership and use as separate
outcomes, which this study does.

2.2 Mobile money, digital payments and development outcomes

The best-known causal evidence comes from Kenya. M-PESA helped households share risk and
sustain consumption after shocks [18], and long-run analysis found that mobile money access
lifted around 2% of households out of poverty, with larger effects among female-headed
households through occupational shifts into business [2]. More broadly, mobile phones reduce
search and coordination costs and improve market information in low-income settings [3]. A
cash-transfer experiment in Niger found that mobile transfers improved household dietary
diversity, partly through time savings relative to manual distribution [19], and debit cards for
Mexican cash-transfer recipients raised savings, partly by reducing transaction costs [20]. Cross-
country work explains differences in mobile-money diffusion by institutional context [21], and
macro evidence links digital financial inclusion with economic growth [22]. In India, biometric
identity-based payments reduced leakage in a large public programme [5], and the 2016
demonetisation has been used to study how digital payments affect consumption [6]. Not every
financial innovation delivers large gains, however: a randomised evaluation of microcredit in
Hyderabad found higher business investment but no significant average gain in consumption,
health or education [23], and a review of digital credit highlights both promise and risk [24]. The
comparison shows that benefits arise when services are actually used, which again motivates
measuring use rather than ownership alone.

2.3 Technology adoption, trust and fintech

The technology acceptance model explains adoption through perceived usefulness and ease of
use [25], and the unified theory extended it with social influence and facilitating conditions [26].
For mobile banking specifically, credibility, self-efficacy and cost shape intention to use [12].
Fintech research frames digital finance as a force that changes industry structure and user

                                                          3
expectations [4], and legal and policy scholarship argues that fintech is a key driver of financial
inclusion and sustainable development, supported by digital identity and interoperable payment
infrastructure [27]. Digital finance has benefits for users, providers and governments, but
persistent issues must be addressed for it to work well [13]. Together, these sources imply that
safety and perceived trust are conditions for continued use, not optional extras.

2.4 Security behaviour, phishing and fraud victimisation

Usable-security research argues that users reject protections that conflict with their tasks, and
that blaming users is unproductive [28]. Experiments show that well-designed fraudulent
websites deceive many participants [10] and interviews with non-expert users show that
awareness of phishing risk is not linked to perceived vulnerability, so people manage familiar
risks but not unfamiliar ones [29]. Interviews and focus groups with online-fraud victims find
that victims are diverse and that scams exploit authority, small sums, pressure and trust [11].
Identity-theft research discusses the prevalence and cost of victimisation and the institutional
setting that makes it possible [30]. Awareness campaigns often fail to change behaviour unless
they are designed around how people act [31], although a survey experiment with a
representative sample of U.S. adults found that short online educational interventions reduced
susceptibility to financial fraud, with effects persisting for at least three months only if a
reminder was provided [32]. This literature supports testing practical, accessible guidance rather
than assuming that general advice protects customers.

2.5 Cyber risk, incentives and policy

Information-security economics explains why security is often under-provided: incentives,
externalities and information asymmetries separate those who bear losses from those who can
prevent them [33]. Economic models of security investment show that optimal spending depends
on vulnerability and potential loss, and need not target the most vulnerable assets [34]. Financial-
sector studies treat cyber risk as a source of market failure and potential instability [35], and
propose principles for regulating it [36]. These sources motivate a shared-responsibility view in
which institutions, regulators and users all contribute to safe digital finance.

2.6 Synthesis and research gap

Three conclusions emerge. First, inclusion benefits depend on use, not just ownership [1, 2, 18].
Second, adoption depends on trust, and security failures exploit ordinary behaviour [10, 11, 12].
Third, security is a shared responsibility that markets alone under-supply [33, 35]. What is
missing is a recent national survey view that places adoption, device protection and reported
scam exposure side by side for India and connects the pattern to an entrepreneurial offering and
its financial viability. This study addresses that gap: it estimates the respondent-level patterns,
and then evaluates a proposed institution-funded service using explicit, testable assumptions.

3. Methodology

The study combines a secondary analysis of survey microdata with a focused review of
institutional documents and a scenario-based financial model. A quantitative, observational
design was chosen because the research questions concern the distribution of, and association
between, adoption and reported exposure in a national population; an experiment or interview
study was not feasible within a term project. Each choice is justified below.

3.1 Research design

The design is cross-sectional, observational and descriptive-analytical. It has five components:
(i) weighted descriptive statistics and subgroup comparisons for RQ1; (ii) an exploratory
weighted logistic regression for RQ2 and a weighted comparison of sending money by lock
status for RQ3; (iii) a side-by-side comparison of payment use and device protection by group
for RQ4; and (iv) a peer-economy comparison of published Findex aggregates and a qualitative
analysis of documents and public app reviews for RQ1 and RQ5; and (v) a deterministic,
assumption-based financial model with scenarios and sensitivity analysis for RQ6. Justification:
a population survey is the only available source that records adoption and exposure for the same
adults; scenario modelling is the standard way to test the viability of a venture before any
commercial data exist.

3.2 Sampling method and participants

The study uses the India file of the Global Findex Database 2025 (survey conducted in 2024,
version IND_2024_FINDEX_v02_M) [9, 37]. The file contains 3,000 respondents aged 15 or
older and 199 variables. Respondents were sampled by the World Bank and Gallup as a
nationally representative adult sample; we did not draw or recruit participants. The supplied
positive survey weight (wgt) corrects for sampling and non-response, and all percentages in this
paper are weighted. Sample sizes and counts of positive responses are unweighted. The data
describe individuals, not a representative sample of businesses. Justification: the survey is the
standard, publicly available benchmark for financial inclusion and is the only source that links
payments to phone-safety items in India.

3.3 Data collection methods

Four data sources were used. (1) Survey microdata. The India microdata were downloaded
from the World Bank microdata library on 4 November 2026 under its access conditions [37]. (2)
Peer-economy aggregates. Published Global Findex 2025 country values for 2024 (account
ownership, mobile phone ownership, phone lock and unsolicited money requests) were retrieved
from the World Bank open data API for India, China, Indonesia, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Brazil,
South Africa, South Asia, lower-middle-income economies and the world [38]; the script
fetch_data.py reproduces the download. (3) Public app reviews. The newest English-language
reviews in the India Google Play store for PhonePe, Google Pay, Paytm, BHIM and SBI YONO
were collected with the google-play-scraper package (up to 3,000 per app; 15,000 retrieved) [39].

                                                          5
Only app, date, rating and text were stored; usernames were not, and email addresses and phone
numbers in text were masked. Reviews shorter than 15 characters and duplicates were removed,
leaving 5,758 reviews posted between 2 October and 19 November 2026. (4) Institutional
documents. Three documents were selected for relevance to trust, shared responsibility and
fraud awareness: the World Bank's discussion of trust [40], its responsible financial access
overview [41] and the Reserve Bank of India's awareness circular [42]. A documented in-app
warning feature [43], the national cyber-fraud helpline [44], published global aggregates [45] and
the NPCI list of live UPI members [46] were used only for context. No interviews, focus groups
or surveys of our own were conducted. Justification: public microdata and open APIs permit
transparent reproduction, and public reviews give unprompted user language that the Findex
questionnaire cannot provide.

3.5 Data analysis

Descriptive analysis. A weighted share is 100 × (sum of weights of positive responses) / (sum of
weights of eligible valid responses). Each subgroup is recalculated with its own eligible
denominator, and percentages and percentage-point gaps are rounded independently from full
precision, so a gap can differ by 0.1 point from subtracting rounded values. Adoption-
protection comparison (RQ4). For each group we place the weighted digital-payment share (all
adults in the group) beside the weighted device-lock share (phone owners in the group) and
report their difference in percentage points. The two shares use different denominators, so the

                               6
difference is descriptive and is not a statistical test. The World Bank dashboard indicator for
women (36%) and men (54%) measures making specified digital payments [47], whereas the
microdata indicator used here covers making or receiving digital payments; the two are different
measures and are not mixed.

Exploratory logistic model. The outcome is con21 (unsolicited request) among phone owners.
Predictors are digital payment use, gender, age (per ten years), education, income quintile,
workforce status and urban residence; age and income enter linearly. All 2,145 eligible
observations have complete inputs. The model is estimated by weighted maximum likelihood
with an independent-observation sandwich covariance. The public file lacks strata and primary
sampling unit identifiers, so the reported intervals are approximate and not design-based.

Peer comparison. Published aggregates are set beside our India estimates without statistical
testing, because sampling errors and country microdata are not available. India's unsolicited-
request share is not published in the API, so the value from our microdata (9.4%) is used and
marked.

Qualitative analysis. Two qualitative sources were analysed. First, passages in the three
institutional documents [40, 41, 42] on adoption barriers, protection mechanisms and
responsibilities were extracted and grouped into themes. Second, the 5,758 app reviews were
coded in two steps. A dictionary of regular expressions flagged five themes (fraud, scam or
unauthorised transactions; money deducted or failed payment; support and complaint handling;
account blocking, verification or login barriers; safety and trust language), and a review could
carry several themes. Because keyword matches can mislead, all 118 reviews that matched the
fraud keywords were then read in full and assigned to one of seven manually defined categories.
A further dictionary counted reviews describing device-lock or security-alert prompts. The
coding was done by a single coder without a second rater, so reliability is untested; the full
classification is supplied so that it can be audited. Review text is treated as unprompted user
opinion, not as verified evidence of fraud.

Financial model. The financial model is deterministic and illustrative. No input is estimated
from Findex. Amounts are in INR lakh (1 lakh = INR 100,000). Table 2 lists the base
assumptions. Three scenarios (conservative, base, optimistic) assume zero starting institutions,
linear growth within each year, no churn and constant scenario pricing of INR 40,000, 50,000
and 60,000 per month. Annual revenue = 12 × average institutions × monthly fee. Annual cost =
fixed cost + 12 × average institutions × monthly variable cost + new institutions × acquisition
cost. Fixed cost grows 20% per year. Tax, interest, depreciation and prelaunch spending are
excluded from operating results.

3.6 Ethical considerations

The study uses de-identified, publicly released microdata under the World Bank's access terms;
no attempt was made to identify respondents, and no personal data were collected. Raw data are
not redistributed with this paper. App reviews are public; no usernames were stored, and short
quotations are anonymous. Responses on unsolicited requests are described as reports, not
verified fraud, and the model is not presented as a customer risk score or as grounds for price
discrimination. The commercial figures are labelled illustrative, and no partner, investor or
willingness-to-pay is claimed. Any future interviews or pilots would require informed consent
and review, and would not collect account credentials.

Justification summary: each method links to a research question: weighted descriptives (RQ1
and RQ4), adjusted association (RQ2), lock-status comparison (RQ3), review coding (RQ5),
scenario and sensitivity modelling (RQ6), with ethics and limits stated so that conclusions stay
within what the data can support.

4.8 Qualitative document review

The review produced three themes. Trust and support: the World Bank's discussion of trust
links trust with awareness of risks and with timely dispute resolution [40]. Shared
responsibility: the World Bank's responsible financial access overview places consumer
protection alongside financial capability [41]. Awareness and credential protection: the
Reserve Bank of India's circular calls for public-awareness campaigns through multiple channels
and highlights protecting credentials such as OTPs and PINs [42]. None of the documents reports
primary data from the respondents in this study, and none evaluates the proposed service.

5. Discussion

The results answer the research questions as follows. For RQ1, ownership is high (89.0%) but
digital use is much lower (48.5%), and both digital use and device protection are lower among

16
women, older adults and lower-income adults. This is consistent with the argument that inclusion
has to be judged by use and not ownership alone [1, 8], and with evidence that gender and
income gaps persist even where access has expanded [8, 9]. Relative to published aggregates
[45], India's account ownership is higher than the worldwide and low- and middle-income rates
while its digital payment use is lower than the low- and middle-income rate, which suggests that
the gap between access and use is wider here than in comparable economies. Against peer
economies (Table 12), India combines near-top account ownership with below-peer phone
ownership, a middling lock share and the lowest reported unsolicited-request share among
economies with a value, which may reflect lower phone-based contact, differences in recall or
reporting, or the smaller share of phone owners. The data cannot separate these explanations.
This comparison is indicative only: it sets our weighted sample estimate (n = 3,000) against
published aggregates without reflecting sampling uncertainty. The survey cannot say why: lower
payment use among women could reflect access to phones, confidence, social norms or the fact
that the indicator counts receipts as well as payments.

For RQ2, payment users reported more unsolicited requests than non-users, and the association
remained after adjustment (odds ratio 3.45). One plausible reading is that people who transact
digitally are reachable through more channels and are more worthwhile targets [30]. It does not
show that digital payments cause fraud, because phone activity, contact lists and reporting
behaviour are unmeasured. The lower odds for women are surprising given their lower digital
use; they may reflect fewer contacts or less willingness to report, which the data cannot separate.
The device-lock result is similarly descriptive. A lock restricts physical access to a handset but
does not protect against deception, which usable-security research shows works through trust and
routine rather than technical failure [10, 11, 28]; for RQ3, the higher share of senders among
locked phones in Table 6 rests on 45 senders and should not be read as a protective or harmful
effect of locking. For RQ4, the largest mismatch is at the oldest ages, where payment use is
relatively high (which may include receiving transfers, since the indicator counts receipts) while
device protection is lowest; among the youngest the pattern is reversed. Because the two shares
use different denominators, this identifies where to test guidance rather than measuring a precise
risk gap. For RQ5, the app reviews show that user grievances are dominated by unpaid rewards
(about half of fraud-keyword reviews), failed or unrefunded payments and weak support or
account-blocking experiences, while reviews describing unauthorised access or theft are very
rare (6 of 5,758). Two readings are plausible: genuine scam victims may not write app-store
reviews, or the apps' reviewers focus on service failures. The reviews therefore do not measure
scam prevalence, but they do show where trust breaks down (refunds, support and verification),
which fits the trust and dispute-resolution themes in the documents [40]. They also show that
safety controls can create friction: 104 reviews describe device-lock or security-alert prompts,
mostly for one app, so a lock requirement that cannot be met or recognised can block legitimate
use. The document review points the same way: guidance that links trust, dispute resolution,
shared responsibility and credential protection supports a service built around understandable
advice and verified routes to support [40, 41, 42]. Its accessibility implication (audio, local-

                                                         17
language and assisted channels) is our design inference from the group differences in Section 4,
not a reported user preference. For RQ6, the model indicates that viability depends mainly on
price, support cost and acquisition efficiency, not on the technical concept, in line with the
economics of security investment [34].

5.1 Academic implications

The study connects two literatures that are usually kept apart: the economics of financial
inclusion [1, 2, 18] and the behavioural study of scams [10, 11, 31]. It shows that national
inclusion statistics can be paired with simple safety indicators to describe where access and safe
use diverge, and it clarifies a measurement point that is easy to miss: the make-or-receive
payment indicator differs from the dashboard's made-payment indicator [47]. Because the public
file lacks design variables, the work also illustrates how interval estimates should be labelled
exploratory when survey structure is unavailable. Finally, it suggests that technology-acceptance
models [12, 25, 26] should include perceived safety and device safeguards as explanatory
variables for continued use among new adopters.

5.2 Policy implications

Three policy implications follow, each framed as a hypothesis for testing. First, inclusion targets
should track active use and safe use, not only account ownership, consistent with the World
Bank's linking of trust and consumer protection with capability [40, 41]. Second, awareness
efforts such as those encouraged by the Reserve Bank of India [42] should be evaluated on
behaviour change and designed for older, lower-income and women users, because generic
campaigns often fail to change behaviour and education effects persisted in one experiment only
when a reminder was provided [31, 32]. Third, shared responsibility implies that providers,
regulators and users each carry duties: providers for in-flow warnings and complaint handling,
regulators for clear reporting channels such as the national helpline [44], and users for credential
hygiene [35, 36]. The findings do not support differential pricing or profiling of demographic
groups.

5.3 Managerial implications

For entrepreneurs and institutional managers, the results suggest a narrowly scoped institutional
pilot: a multilingual payment-safety companion distributed through banks, payment providers or
merchant networks. The paying customer would be the institution; end users would not pay. The
service would offer short audio and visual explanations, a pre-payment checklist and verified
routes to the institution's support and official reporting channels. It would not hold money,
authorise payments, guarantee recovery or request credentials such as an OTP or PIN. Table 15

5.4 Future scope and limitations

Limitations. The survey is cross-sectional and self-reported, so it cannot establish causation,
timing or verified losses. Unsolicited requests are not confirmed fraud, and sending money is not
a measured loss amount. Phone-owner and request-recipient analyses concern subsets of the
sample, and subgroup cells such as the 45 senders are small. Intervals are approximate because
design variables are missing. The data describe individuals, not enterprises. The qualitative
component rests on a secondary document review and on public app reviews, which are self-
selected, cover seven weeks, come from five apps with unequal review counts, are English-
language and keyword-coded by a single coder, and are not linked to Findex respondents. No
interviews or surveys of our own were conducted. Peer values are published aggregates without
sampling errors. All financial inputs are unvalidated assumptions with no churn, tax or financing
flows.

Future scope. Priority extensions are (i) consent-based interviews with merchants, first-time
users and institutional budget owners to test willingness to pay; (ii) design-based estimation if
strata and cluster identifiers become available; (iii) pooling Findex rounds [8, 9, 15] and
extending the comparison from published aggregates to country-level microdata for peer
economies; (iv) an evaluated pilot with pre-specified outcomes and a power calculation before
treating verified loss as an outcome [32]; and (v) extending the venture model with churn, taxes,
receivable timing and month-by-month cash flow.

6. Conclusion

This paper set out to understand how mobile banking inclusion and cybersecurity exposure
coexist in India, and what that means for an entrepreneurial response. Using the 2024 India
Global Findex, it found that account ownership is widespread (89.0%) but digital payment use
(48.5%) and device protection (57.7% of phone owners) are lower and unevenly distributed by
gender, age and income. Payment users reported more unsolicited money requests than non-users
(13.1% versus 4.3%), and the association remained after adjustment (odds ratio 3.45). The
literature shows that benefits of digital finance depend on use and trust, and that scams exploit
ordinary behaviour; the results are consistent with both ideas, although they do not prove
causation.

For entrepreneurs and institutions, the evidence supports testing an accessible, institution-funded
payment-safety service in a staged pilot. An illustrative model reaches steady-state break-even at

                               21
eight paying institutions and a three-year surplus in the base case, but the result is highly
sensitive to price, support cost, acquisition cost and renewal, and it is not evidence of demand.
App reviews added that user grievances concern rewards, refunds, support and verification far
more than third-party fraud, and a peer comparison placed India near the top on account
ownership but below peers on phone ownership. The contribution of the study is therefore a
transparent description of where access and safe use diverge, and a validated-assumption-first
path from that pattern to a testable venture. Pilot evidence on willingness to pay, retention and
effectiveness is the necessary next step.
