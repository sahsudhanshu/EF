[Tit] Mobile Banking: Financial Inclusion vs Cybersecurity Risks
[Nor] Evidence from India's 2024 Global Findex, public app reviews and published interview studies, with implications for entrepreneurs
[Nor] Rushal Verma (IIT2024045), Vichanshu Raj (IIT2024083) and Sudhanshu (IIT2024081)
[Nor] Group 31, Entrepreneurial Finance
[Nor] 20 November 2026
[Hea] Abstract
[Nor] This study asks whether the spread of mobile banking and digital payments in India comes with higher cybersecurity risk, and for whom, and what this implies for entrepreneurs who serve first-time digital users. We analyse the 2024 India Global Findex survey of 3,000 adults with survey weights and an exploratory logistic model, compare India with peer economies, code 14,088 public reviews of 13 payment and bank apps from two app stores, and synthesise five published interview studies. Account ownership was 89.0%, but only 48.5% made or received a digital payment and 57.7% of phone owners reported a device lock. Payment users reported more unsolicited money requests than non-users (13.1% against 4.3%; adjusted odds ratio 3.45), yet exposure was also high in the lowest income quintile and protection lagged inclusion at older ages. Reviews rarely described theft: payment-app complaints concerned rewards and refunds, bank-app complaints concerned account blocks, charges and security prompts. An illustrative model for an institution-funded safety service breaks even at eight paying institutions. The data cannot show causation, and the commercial assumptions need pilot validation.
[Nor] Keywords: financial inclusion; mobile banking; digital payments; cybersecurity; fraud exposure; Global Findex; entrepreneurial finance; India
[Hea] 1. Introduction
[Hea] 1.1 Background
[Nor] Access to formal finance is widely linked with household resilience, enterprise formation and poverty reduction [1, 2]. Over the past decade, mobile phones and digital payment networks have changed how that access is delivered, allowing people to pay, save and transfer money without visiting a branch [3, 4]. India is a prominent example: its public digital identity and payment infrastructure have been studied for effects on service delivery and household consumption [5, 6]. For a small business owner or a first-time digital customer, a bank account is useful only if it can be used reliably to receive sales proceeds, pay suppliers and manage cash, and only if it can be used without undue risk.
[Nor] Three terms are used throughout. Financial inclusion is the use of formal financial services. Account ownership measures access, whereas digital payment use measures activity. Reported cybersecurity exposure, in this study, means a person's report of an unsolicited call or message asking for money; it is a measure of exposure to a possible scam attempt, not verified fraud. The World Bank's Global Findex is the most widely used cross-country source for the first two measures [7, 8, 9].
[Hea] 1.2 Problem statement and importance
[Nor] Account ownership, active digital use and safe digital use are different dimensions of inclusion. A population that holds accounts but uses them little, or uses them without basic safeguards, can be left exposed even as headline inclusion rises. Evidence from fraud and phishing research shows that deception exploits ordinary behaviour rather than ignorance alone [10, 11], so the risk grows as new, less experienced users enter digital channels. For entrepreneurs and financial institutions the issue is practical: perceived credibility and trust influence whether customers adopt mobile banking [12], and digital finance brings benefits alongside unresolved issues [13].
[Nor] This paper therefore studies the tension in its title. Mobile banking can bring into the formal system people who were outside it, but each new user is also a new target and may lack the habits and safeguards of experienced users. The question is not whether digital finance is good or bad, but where inclusion and cybersecurity risk move together, where they do not, and what institutions and entrepreneurs could do about it.
[Hea] 1.3 Research gap
[Nor] The existing literature is strong on the economic benefits of mobile money and on the behavioural causes of phishing, but the two strands are rarely joined in a single, recent, nationally broad survey of India. Studies of digital inclusion seldom report device-level safeguards or reported scam exposure, and security research seldom reports representative adoption patterns by gender, age and income. Little work also translates such respondent-level patterns into an entrepreneurial finance question: what service could be built, who would pay for it, and what financial conditions would make it viable. This paper addresses these gaps with the 2024 India Global Findex microdata and a transparent, assumption-labelled venture model.
[Hea] 1.4 Research questions
[Nor] Taken together, the six questions below ask whether more inclusion means more cybersecurity risk, for whom, and what can be done.
[Nor] RQ1. How do account ownership, digital payment use and device protection differ across women and men, age groups and income quintiles in India, and how does India compare with peer economies?
[Nor] RQ2. Are digital payment users more likely than non-users to report unsolicited money requests, after adjusting for demographic and economic characteristics?
[Nor] RQ3. Among people who report an unsolicited money request, is having a device lock associated with a lower share reporting that they sent money?
[Nor] RQ4. Which groups show the largest gap between digital payment use and device protection?
[Nor] RQ5. What do public app reviews, institutional documents and published interview studies reveal about fraud-related grievances, security friction and support experiences?
[Nor] RQ6. What financial conditions (price, costs and customer numbers) would allow an institution-funded payment-safety service to break even, and how sensitive is that result to its assumptions?
[Nor] RQ1 to RQ4 refine the four questions in our progress report (inclusion and digital use, exposure of users, protective practices, and groups with the largest gap); RQ5 and RQ6 were added when the qualitative and venture components were developed.
[Hea] 2. Literature review
[Nor] This review covers the economics of financial inclusion and mobile money, technology adoption and trust in mobile banking, security behaviour and fraud, the economics of cyber risk, and the entrepreneurial and policy literature on digital financial services. It follows the history of the field, compares findings across sources and ends by identifying the gap this study fills. It draws on 35 academic and policy sources; institutional documents used for context are cited in Sections 3 to 5.
[Hea] 2.1 Financial inclusion, development and measurement
[Nor] Cross-country work established that deeper finance disproportionately raises the incomes of the poorest and reduces inequality [14], and later reviews found that account access supports inclusive growth, although the effects depend on how accounts are used [1]. Measurement advanced with the first Global Findex in 2011 [7] and its repeated rounds [8, 9, 15], which separated account ownership from usage. Index-based studies had earlier ranked countries on banking penetration, availability and usage and associated inclusion with income, literacy, urbanisation and infrastructure [16]. Financial illiteracy is widespread across the countries studied [17]. Taken together, these sources justify treating ownership and use as separate outcomes, which this study does.
[Hea] 2.2 Mobile money, digital payments and development outcomes
[Nor] The best-known causal evidence comes from Kenya. M-PESA helped households share risk and sustain consumption after shocks [18], and long-run analysis found that mobile money access lifted around 2% of households out of poverty, with larger effects among female-headed households through occupational shifts into business [2]. More broadly, mobile phones reduce search and coordination costs and improve market information in low-income settings [3]. A cash-transfer experiment in Niger found that mobile transfers improved household dietary diversity, partly through time savings relative to manual distribution [19], and debit cards for Mexican cash-transfer recipients raised savings, partly by reducing transaction costs [20]. Cross-country work explains differences in mobile-money diffusion by institutional context [21], and macro evidence links digital financial inclusion with economic growth [22]. In India, biometric identity-based payments reduced leakage in a large public programme [5], and the 2016 demonetisation has been used to study how digital payments affect consumption [6]. Not every financial innovation delivers large gains, however: a randomised evaluation of microcredit in Hyderabad found higher business investment but no significant average gain in consumption, health or education [23], and a review of digital credit highlights both promise and risk [24]. The comparison shows that benefits arise when services are actually used, which again motivates measuring use rather than ownership alone.
[Hea] 2.3 Technology adoption, trust and fintech
[Nor] The technology acceptance model explains adoption through perceived usefulness and ease of use [25], and the unified theory extended it with social influence and facilitating conditions [26]. For mobile banking specifically, credibility, self-efficacy and cost shape intention to use [12]. Fintech research frames digital finance as a force that changes industry structure and user expectations [4], and legal and policy scholarship argues that fintech is a key driver of financial inclusion and sustainable development, supported by digital identity and interoperable payment infrastructure [27]. Digital finance has benefits for users, providers and governments, but persistent issues must be addressed for it to work well [13]. Together, these sources imply that safety and perceived trust are conditions for continued use, not optional extras.
[Hea] 2.4 Security behaviour, phishing and fraud victimisation
[Nor] Usable-security research argues that users reject protections that conflict with their tasks, and that blaming users is unproductive [28]. Experiments show that well-designed fraudulent websites deceive many participants [10] and interviews with non-expert users show that awareness of phishing risk is not linked to perceived vulnerability, so people manage familiar risks but not unfamiliar ones [29]. Interviews and focus groups with online-fraud victims find that victims are diverse and that scams exploit authority, small sums, pressure and trust [11]. Identity-theft research discusses the prevalence and cost of victimisation and the institutional setting that makes it possible [30]. Awareness campaigns often fail to change behaviour unless they are designed around how people act [31], although a survey experiment with a representative sample of U.S. adults found that short online educational interventions reduced susceptibility to financial fraud, with effects persisting for at least three months only if a reminder was provided [32]. This literature supports testing practical, accessible guidance rather than assuming that general advice protects customers.
[Hea] 2.5 Cyber risk, incentives and policy
[Nor] Information-security economics explains why security is often under-provided: incentives, externalities and information asymmetries separate those who bear losses from those who can prevent them [33]. Economic models of security investment show that optimal spending depends on vulnerability and potential loss, and need not target the most vulnerable assets [34]. Financial-sector studies treat cyber risk as a source of market failure and potential instability [35], and propose principles for regulating it [36]. These sources motivate a shared-responsibility view in which institutions, regulators and users all contribute to safe digital finance.
[Hea] 2.6 Synthesis and research gap
[Nor] Three conclusions emerge. First, inclusion benefits depend on use, not just ownership [1, 2, 18]. Second, adoption depends on trust, and security failures exploit ordinary behaviour [10, 11, 12]. Third, security is a shared responsibility that markets alone under-supply [33, 35]. What is missing is a recent national survey view that places adoption, device protection and reported scam exposure side by side for India and connects the pattern to an entrepreneurial offering and its financial viability. This study addresses that gap: it estimates the respondent-level patterns, and then evaluates a proposed institution-funded service using explicit, testable assumptions.
[Hea] 3. Methodology
[Nor] The study combines a secondary analysis of survey microdata with a focused review of institutional documents and a scenario-based financial model. A quantitative, observational design was chosen because the research questions concern the distribution of, and association between, adoption and reported exposure in a national population; an experiment or interview study of our own was not feasible within a term project, so published interview studies are used as secondary evidence. Each choice is justified below.
[Hea] 3.1 Research design
[Nor] The design is cross-sectional, observational and descriptive-analytical. It has five components: (i) weighted descriptive statistics and subgroup comparisons for RQ1; (ii) an exploratory weighted logistic regression for RQ2 and a weighted comparison of sending money by lock status for RQ3; (iii) a side-by-side comparison of payment use and device protection by group for RQ4; (iv) a peer-economy comparison of published Findex aggregates and a qualitative analysis of documents, public app reviews and published interview studies for RQ1 and RQ5; and (v) a deterministic, assumption-based financial model with scenarios and sensitivity analysis for RQ6. Justification: a population survey is the only available source that records adoption and exposure for the same adults; scenario modelling is the standard way to test the viability of a venture before any commercial data exist.
[Hea] 3.2 Sampling method and participants
[Nor] The study uses the India file of the Global Findex Database 2025 (survey conducted in 2024, version IND_2024_FINDEX_v02_M) [9, 37]. The file contains 3,000 respondents aged 15 or older and 199 variables. Respondents were sampled by the World Bank and Gallup as a nationally representative adult sample; we did not draw or recruit participants. The supplied positive survey weight (wgt) corrects for sampling and non-response, and all percentages in this paper are weighted. Sample sizes and counts of positive responses are unweighted. The data describe individuals, not a representative sample of businesses. Justification: the survey is the standard, publicly available benchmark for financial inclusion and is the only source that links payments to phone-safety items in India.
[Hea] 3.3 Data collection methods
[Nor] Seven data sources were used. (1) Survey microdata. The India microdata were downloaded from the World Bank microdata library on 4 November 2026 under its access conditions [37]. (2) Peer-economy aggregates. Published Global Findex 2025 country values for 2024 (account ownership, mobile phone ownership, phone lock and unsolicited money requests) were retrieved from the World Bank open data API for India, China, Indonesia, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Brazil, South Africa, South Asia, lower-middle-income economies and the world [38]; the script fetch_data.py reproduces the download. The progress report planned to use the aggregate Global Findex database file for this context; because only four indicators for ten economies were needed, the same published Findex 2025 indicators were retrieved from the API instead. (3) Public app reviews. The newest English-language reviews in the India Google Play store for PhonePe, Google Pay, Paytm, BHIM and SBI YONO were collected with the google-play-scraper package (up to 3,000 per app; 15,000 retrieved) [39]. Only app, date, rating and text were stored; usernames were not, and email addresses and phone numbers in text were masked. Reviews shorter than 15 characters and duplicates were removed, leaving 5,758 reviews posted between 2 October and 19 November 2026. (4) Institutional documents. Three documents were selected for relevance to trust, shared responsibility and fraud awareness: the World Bank's discussion of trust [40], its responsible financial access overview [41] and the Reserve Bank of India's awareness circular [42]. A documented in-app warning feature [43], the national cyber-fraud helpline [44], published global aggregates [45] and the Government of India's UPI factsheet [46] (741 live banks in July 2026) were used only for context. (5) Apple App Store reviews. To test whether the Google Play pattern holds on another platform, public customer reviews of the same five apps in the India App Store were retrieved from Apple's customer-review feed [47]: 2,090 reviews remained after removing reviews shorter than 15 characters and duplicates, posted between 30 April and 18 November 2026 (the feed returns only the most recent reviews per app, so older reviews of BHIM and SBI YONO are included). Only app, date, rating, version, title and text were stored; reviewer names were not. (6) Bank mobile-banking app reviews. Because mobile banking is delivered mainly through banks' own apps, we also collected public reviews of eight bank apps: the newest English-language Google Play reviews of ICICI iMobile, Axis Mobile, Kotak Bank, bob World, Canara ai1 and IDFC FIRST Bank (up to 1,500 per app) and App Store reviews of HDFC Bank, ICICI iMobile, Axis Mobile, Kotak 811, PNB ONE and bob World (the newest 500 or fewer per app), with google-play-scraper and Apple's customer-review feed [48]. Only app, date, rating and text (and title on the App Store) were stored; reviewer names were not, and email addresses and phone numbers were masked. After removing reviews shorter than 15 characters and duplicates, 6,240 reviews remained (4,279 Google Play, 1,961 App Store), posted between 5 June and 19 November 2026; HDFC Bank's App Store feed returned only 100. (7) Published interview studies. Because we conducted no interviews of our own, five published qualitative studies of Indian digital-payment users, merchants and fraud victims were used as secondary evidence: 26 user interviews with an analysis of official advice [49]; 46 scenario-based interviews on fraudulent payment requests [50]; observations and interviews with 24 low-income merchants and 10 payment agents [51]; a qualitative study of 50 users in Delhi with 10 institutional stakeholders [52]; and a mixed-methods study with 18 interviews, 7 focus groups and a survey of 3,784 users [53]. They were found through targeted searches of publisher and institutional sites on 20 November 2026 and chosen because they are India-specific, concern fraud, trust or support in digital payments, and are openly accessible. They are not a systematic review. We conducted no interviews, focus groups or surveys of our own. Justification: public microdata and open APIs permit transparent reproduction, public reviews give unprompted user language that the Findex questionnaire cannot provide, and published interview studies supply the experiences of victims, merchants and institution staff that reviews rarely contain.

[Hea] 3.5 Data analysis
[Nor] Descriptive analysis. A weighted share is 100 × (sum of weights of positive responses) / (sum of weights of eligible valid responses). Each subgroup is recalculated with its own eligible denominator, and percentages and percentage-point gaps are rounded independently from full precision, so a gap can differ by 0.1 point from subtracting rounded values. Adoption-protection comparison (RQ4). For each group we place the weighted digital-payment share (all adults in the group) beside the weighted device-lock share (phone owners in the group) and report their difference in percentage points. The two shares use different denominators, so the difference is descriptive and is not a statistical test. The World Bank dashboard indicator for women (36%) and men (54%) measures making specified digital payments [54], whereas the microdata indicator used here covers making or receiving digital payments; the two are different measures and are not mixed.
[Nor] Exploratory logistic model. The outcome is con21 (unsolicited request) among phone owners. Predictors are digital payment use, gender, age (per ten years), education, income quintile, workforce status and urban residence; age and income enter linearly. All 2,145 eligible observations have complete inputs. The model is estimated by weighted maximum likelihood with an independent-observation sandwich covariance. The public file lacks strata and primary sampling unit identifiers, so the reported intervals are approximate and not design-based. As a sensitivity check, the Kish design effect from the weights alone and the effect of assuming additional clustering (variance inflated by factors of 1.5, 2 and 3) were computed.
[Nor] Peer comparison. Published aggregates are set beside our India estimates without statistical testing, because sampling errors and country microdata are not available. India's unsolicited-request share is not published in the API, so the value from our microdata (9.4%) is used and marked.
[Nor] Inclusion and exposure by group. For each of 18 demographic groups (sex, age, education, income quintile, residence and work) we place the weighted digital-payment share among all adults in the group (inclusion) beside the weighted share of phone owners who reported an unsolicited money request (risk) and the device-lock share (protection), and report Spearman rank correlations across groups. Groups overlap and the denominators differ, so this is descriptive and is not a statistical test.
[Nor] Significance tests. Weighted shares are descriptive, and no design-based test is possible without strata or cluster identifiers. For the lock-status comparison among request recipients (RQ3) we report a Fisher exact test on the unweighted counts. For the review data we compare bank and payment apps with two-sample proportion tests (chi-square) for each of the six dictionary themes, with Holm adjustment for the six tests, and with Fisher exact tests for the share of fraud-keyword reviews about rewards and about blocked accounts, mis-selling or grievances. These tests treat reviews as independent observations from a self-selected source, so p-values indicate whether differences are larger than chance variation in the sample, not whether they generalise to all users.
[Nor] Qualitative analysis. Five qualitative sources were analysed. First, passages in the three institutional documents [40, 41, 42] on adoption barriers, protection mechanisms and responsibilities were extracted and grouped into themes. Second, the 5,758 app reviews were coded in two steps. A dictionary of regular expressions flagged five themes (fraud, scam or unauthorised transactions; money deducted or failed payment; support and complaint handling; account blocking, verification or login barriers; safety and trust language), and a review could carry several themes. Because keyword matches can mislead, all 118 reviews that matched the fraud keywords were then read in full and assigned to one of seven manually defined categories. A further dictionary counted reviews describing device-lock or security-alert prompts. The first pass was done by a single coder. To check consistency, all 118 reviews were then re-classified in a second pass made without sight of the first labels; the two passes agreed on 112 of 118 reviews (94.9%; Cohen's kappa 0.93), and the six disagreements did not change the unauthorised-access total (6 in both passes) or the reward and cashback total (60 against 59). Both of those passes were AI-assisted. A human coder from outside the group, who had not seen the first-pass labels, then independently classified a random sample of 40 of the 118 reviews, stratified by first-pass category; agreement with the first pass was 95.0% (38 of 40; Cohen's kappa 0.93). The two disagreements were a review about missing support coded as a general accusation rather than poor grievance handling, and a review about a complaint for an unauthorised transaction coded as poor grievance handling rather than unauthorised access. The same coder judged that none of 60 randomly chosen reviews that the fraud keywords had not matched described fraud or unauthorised use. With only 40 reviews the agreement estimate is imprecise. The full classification and the second-pass labels are supplied so that they can be audited. Review text is treated as unprompted user opinion, not as verified evidence of fraud. Apple App Store reviews were coded with the same dictionary and category scheme; the 57 fraud-keyword reviews were classified by one coder and then re-classified in a blind second pass by a separate AI instance that saw only the codebook and the review text; the passes agreed on 47 of 57 reviews (82.5%; Cohen's kappa 0.78), with the disagreements concentrated in the blocked-account, mis-selling and grievance category. Bank mobile-banking app reviews were coded with the same five themes and a further dictionary for security-friction prompts (device or SIM binding, root, developer-option or unauthorised-app checks, screen-sharing, remote-access and VPN blocks, biometric prompts, and security alerts). The 65 fraud-keyword bank-app reviews were classified into the seven categories by one coder and re-classified in a blind second pass by a separate AI instance, which agreed on 57 of 65 (87.7%; kappa 0.84). To compare bank and payment apps, the same dictionaries were applied to all 7,848 payment-app reviews. Published studies. For each of the five published studies we extracted the findings on adoption, scams, trust, support and redress and mapped them to eight themes defined from our research questions (S1 to S8, Table 19). A theme was recorded for a study only when its text reported it, and counts are of studies (maximum five), not of respondents. Extraction was done by one coder from the full text of all five studies; no participant quotations are reproduced.
[Nor] Financial model. The financial model is deterministic and illustrative. No input is estimated from Findex. Amounts are in INR lakh (1 lakh = INR 100,000). Table 2 lists the base assumptions. Three scenarios (conservative, base, optimistic) assume zero starting institutions, linear growth within each year, no churn and constant scenario pricing of INR 40,000, 50,000 and 60,000 per month. Annual revenue = 12 × average institutions × monthly fee. Annual cost = fixed cost + 12 × average institutions × monthly variable cost + new institutions × acquisition cost. Fixed cost grows 20% per year. Tax, interest, depreciation and prelaunch spending are excluded from operating results.

[Hea] 3.6 Ethical considerations
[Nor] The study uses de-identified, publicly released microdata under the World Bank's access terms; no attempt was made to identify respondents, and no personal data were collected. Raw data are not redistributed with this paper. App reviews from both stores are public; no usernames were stored, and short quotations are anonymous. The published interview studies are cited and summarised, not reproduced. Responses on unsolicited requests are described as reports, not verified fraud, and the model is not presented as a customer risk score or as grounds for price discrimination. The commercial figures are labelled illustrative, and no partner, investor or willingness-to-pay is claimed. Any future interviews or pilots would require informed consent and review, and would not collect account credentials.
[Nor] Justification summary: each method links to a research question: weighted descriptives (RQ1 and RQ4), adjusted association (RQ2), lock-status comparison (RQ3), review coding and the secondary synthesis (RQ5), scenario and sensitivity modelling (RQ6), with ethics and limits stated so that conclusions stay within what the data can support.
[Hea] 4. Results
[Nor] All percentages are survey-weighted. Counts and valid n are unweighted. This section reports findings without interpretation.
[Hea] 4.1 Adoption and exposure
[Nor] Account ownership was 89.0% and digital payment use was 48.5% among all 3,000 adults; the difference is 40.6 percentage points. Phone ownership was 66.5%. Digital payment use was 42.7% for women and 54.1% for men, a gap of 11.5 percentage points. Among phone owners, 9.4% reported an unsolicited money request: 13.1% of payment users and 4.3% of non-users. Of 219 request recipients, 21.0% reported sending money (Table 3, Figure 1).

[Hea] 4.3 Adjusted association with request exposure
[Nor] The unadjusted difference in reported requests between payment users and non-users was 8.8 percentage points (Figure 3). In the adjusted model (2,145 phone owners; 219 positive outcomes), the odds ratio for payment users versus non-users was 3.45 (approximate 95% interval 2.24 to 5.30) and for women versus men 0.47 (0.32 to 0.69). Tertiary versus primary education or less had an odds ratio of 1.70 (1.03 to 2.80). Age, income, workforce status and urban residence had intervals that included 1.00 (Table 5). The weights alone give a Kish design effect of 1.43 (effective sample 1,501 of 2,145). When the variance is inflated for assumed extra design effects of 1.5, 2 and 3, the interval for payment users stays above 1.00 (1.64 to 7.26 at 3) and the interval for women stays below 1.00 (0.24 to 0.92 at 3), but the interval for tertiary education includes 1.00 from an extra effect of 1.5 onwards.

[Hea] 5. Discussion
[Nor] The results answer the research questions as follows. For RQ1, ownership is high (89.0%) but digital use is much lower (48.5%); digital payment use is lower among women and lower-income adults, and device protection is lower among women, older adults and lower-income adults (payment use is highest, not lowest, at ages 60 and above). This is consistent with the argument that inclusion has to be judged by use and not ownership alone [1, 8], and with evidence that gender and income gaps persist even where access has expanded [8, 9]. Relative to published aggregates [45], India's account ownership is higher than the worldwide and low- and middle-income rates while its digital payment use is lower than the low- and middle-income rate, which suggests that the gap between access and use is wider here than in comparable economies. Against peer economies (Table 12), India combines near-top account ownership with below-peer phone ownership, a middling lock share and the lowest reported unsolicited-request share among economies with a value, which may reflect lower phone-based contact, differences in recall or reporting, or the smaller share of phone owners. The data cannot separate these explanations. This comparison is indicative only: it sets our weighted sample estimate (n = 3,000) against published aggregates without reflecting sampling uncertainty. The survey cannot say why: lower payment use among women could reflect access to phones, confidence, social norms or the fact that the indicator counts receipts as well as payments.
[Nor] For RQ2, payment users reported more unsolicited requests than non-users, and the association remained after adjustment (odds ratio 3.45). One plausible reading is that people who transact digitally are reachable through more channels and are more worthwhile targets [30]. It does not show that digital payments cause fraud, because phone activity, contact lists and reporting behaviour are unmeasured. The lower odds for women are surprising given their lower digital use; they may reflect fewer contacts or less willingness to report, which the data cannot separate. The device-lock result is similarly descriptive. A lock restricts physical access to a handset but does not protect against deception, which usable-security research shows works through trust and routine rather than technical failure [10, 11, 28]; for RQ3, the higher share of senders among locked phones in Table 6 rests on 45 senders and should not be read as a protective or harmful effect of locking. For RQ4, the largest mismatch is at the oldest ages, where payment use is relatively high (which may include receiving transfers, since the indicator counts receipts) while device protection is lowest; among the youngest the pattern is reversed. Because the two shares use different denominators, this identifies where to test guidance rather than measuring a precise risk gap. For RQ5, the app reviews show that user grievances are dominated by unpaid rewards (about half of fraud-keyword reviews), failed or unrefunded payments and weak support or account-blocking experiences, while reviews describing unauthorised access or theft are very rare (6 of 5,758). Two readings are plausible: genuine scam victims may not write app-store reviews, or the apps' reviewers focus on service failures. The reviews therefore do not measure scam prevalence, but they do show where trust breaks down (refunds, support and verification), which fits the trust and dispute-resolution themes in the documents [40]. They also show that safety controls can create friction: 104 reviews describe device-lock or security-alert prompts, mostly for one app, so a lock requirement that cannot be met or recognised can block legitimate use. The document review points the same way: guidance that links trust, dispute resolution, shared responsibility and credential protection supports a service built around understandable advice and verified routes to support [40, 41, 42]. Its accessibility implication (audio, local-language and assisted channels) is our design inference from the group differences in Section 4, not a reported user preference. For RQ6, the model indicates that viability depends mainly on price, support cost and acquisition efficiency, not on the technical concept, in line with the economics of security investment [34].
[Nor] Inclusion versus cybersecurity risk. The central question of the paper has a mixed answer. Digital payment users reported more unsolicited requests than non-users (13.1% against 4.3%) and the association survived adjustment, so inclusion in digital payments goes with greater reachability by scammers. Across groups, however, the link was weak to moderate (rank correlation 0.38 between payment use and exposure; Table 8 and Figure 4): exposure was highest among tertiary-educated adults, who are the most included, but also in the lowest income quintile, which is among the least included, and lowest at ages 60 and above and among women. Device protection rose with inclusion (rank correlation 0.63) except where the two diverge, notably among older adults, whose payment use (56.6%) far exceeds lock use (22.9%). Risk is therefore not simply the price of inclusion; it concentrates where inclusion outruns safeguards and where people have little margin for loss. These are descriptive patterns, and differences in reporting between groups may contribute to them.
[Nor] Bank apps and payment apps. Bank-app reviews differ from payment-app reviews in what they complain about. Fraud keywords were rarer (1.0% against 2.2%) and, once read, were mostly about blocked accounts, unexpected charges, mis-selling and unreachable support (38.5% of fraud-keyword reviews) rather than unpaid rewards (4.6%, against 50.8% in the Google Play payment-app reviews), and spam or intrusive security controls were common (20.0%). Security-friction prompts such as SIM binding, biometric set-up and developer-option or unauthorised-app detection were a recurring source of one-star reviews, which fits the observation that safety controls can lock out legitimate users. Reports of theft remained very rare (3 to 4 of 6,240). In bank apps the tension between inclusion and risk therefore shows up less as fraud than as friction and weak redress, which supports a service built around understandable guidance and verified routes to support.

[Nor] Evidence beyond Google Play. The App Store reviews repeat the Google Play pattern: unpaid rewards, failed payments, grievance handling and spam outnumber reviews of unauthorised access (2 of 2,090 in the first coding pass and 4 in the second), so the finding does not depend on one platform. The five published interview studies (Tables 18 and 19) describe a different part of the problem. Social engineering through calls, links, OTP requests, fake payment requests, impersonation and tampered QR codes appears in all five studies, and trust in the counterparty or the bank appears in all five. This is consistent with the possibility that people who lose money to scams seldom write app-store reviews: the harms interview participants describe are largely absent from the review data, while support and redress failures appear in both sources. Two details bear on the proposed service. Participants in one study rated a recipient trust badge highest of four nudges [50], and participants in another preferred bank-integrated apps because problems could be raised with the bank [49]; both point to verified cues and verified routes to the institution, which the proposed design includes. Family and WhatsApp were prominent information sources in two studies [49, 52], which suggests testing distribution through those channels. These studies are secondary evidence from convenience or purposive samples, were not designed to answer our questions, and say nothing about willingness to pay.
[Hea] 5.1 Academic implications
[Nor] The study connects two literatures that are usually kept apart: the economics of financial inclusion [1, 2, 18] and the behavioural study of scams [10, 11, 31]. It shows that national inclusion statistics can be paired with simple safety indicators to describe where access and safe use diverge, and it clarifies a measurement point that is easy to miss: the make-or-receive payment indicator differs from the dashboard's made-payment indicator [54]. Because the public file lacks design variables, the work also illustrates how interval estimates should be labelled exploratory when survey structure is unavailable. Finally, it suggests that technology-acceptance models [12, 25, 26] should include perceived safety and device safeguards as explanatory variables for continued use among new adopters.
[Hea] 5.2 Policy implications
[Nor] Three policy implications follow, each framed as a hypothesis for testing. First, inclusion targets should track active use and safe use, not only account ownership, consistent with the World Bank's linking of trust and consumer protection with capability [40, 41]. Second, awareness efforts such as those encouraged by the Reserve Bank of India [42] should be evaluated on behaviour change and designed for older, lower-income and women users, because generic campaigns often fail to change behaviour and education effects persisted in one experiment only when a reminder was provided [31, 32]. Third, shared responsibility implies that providers, regulators and users each carry duties: providers for in-flow warnings and complaint handling, regulators for clear reporting channels such as the national helpline [44], and users for credential hygiene [35, 36]. The findings do not support differential pricing or profiling of demographic groups.
[Hea] 5.3 Managerial implications
[Nor] For entrepreneurs and institutional managers, the results suggest a narrowly scoped institutional pilot: a multilingual payment-safety companion distributed through banks, payment providers or merchant networks. The paying customer would be the institution; end users would not pay. The service would offer short audio and visual explanations, a pre-payment checklist and verified routes to the institution's support and official reporting channels. It would not hold money, authorise payments, guarantee recovery or request credentials such as an OTP or PIN. Table 21 sets out the business model canvas.

[Nor] Existing alternatives already cover parts of the problem: bank and RBI awareness campaigns [42], in-app warnings such as those described for Google Pay [43], bank support and official reporting through the national portal and helpline 1930 [44], and an institution building the service itself. The service must therefore add measurable value: partner-specific local-language journeys with comprehension checks, practice outside the transaction flow, faster routes to verified support without duplicating official investigation, and reusable localised content that beats the buyer's internal cost and quality. This is a functional review of documented alternatives, not a scored market survey, and the effectiveness of each addition must be tested.
[Nor] Go-to-market. A staged plan is proposed. In months 1–2 (discovery and design), secure one sponsor institution and a named budget owner, and conduct consent-based usability sessions using simulated scenarios. In months 3–5 (controlled pilot), compare the companion with existing guidance and prespecify outcomes: comprehension on unseen scenarios, identification of official support, completion and legitimate-task delay. From month 6 (commercial gate), scale only after a paid commitment, manageable support load and acceptable security review. These are proposed steps; no pilot has been run.
[Nor] Financial interpretation. Under the stated assumptions, eight institutions cover fixed costs only in steady state, while growth requires about 13 because acquisition is expensed (Table 9). Pricing power matters: a 20% lower fee increases the requirement by two institutions, and discounting consumes the margin that funds acquisition (Table 11). The base case offers no cushion in Year 2, so churn or slow sales would turn it to deficit; the conservative case does not break even within three years. Revenue is also not cash: procurement and collection delays could create a gap even when operating results improve. These conclusions follow the logic of security-investment economics and incentive problems [33, 34], and they hold only if prices and costs are validated with real buyers.

[Hea] 5.4 Future scope and limitations
[Nor] Limitations. The survey is cross-sectional and self-reported, so it cannot establish causation, timing or verified losses. Unsolicited requests are not confirmed fraud, and sending money is not a measured loss amount. Phone-owner and request-recipient analyses concern subsets of the sample, and subgroup cells such as the 45 senders are small. Intervals are approximate because design variables are missing; a sensitivity analysis (Section 4.3) shows that the payment-user and gender results survive large assumed clustering effects while the tertiary-education result does not. The data describe individuals, not enterprises. The qualitative component rests on a secondary document review and on public app reviews, which are self-selected, cover seven weeks, come from five apps with unequal review counts, are English-language and keyword-coded by a single coder, checked by an AI second pass for all 118 fraud-keyword reviews and by a human coder for a sample of 40 (so human reliability rests on a small sample), and are not linked to Findex respondents. The keyword dictionaries are English only; a Hindi and Hinglish extension (Section 4.13) added 41 reviews and left the conclusions unchanged. Bank-app reviews cover eight apps over about five months, the newest reviews only (HDFC Bank's App Store feed returned 100), and were checked only by an AI second pass (87.7% agreement). We conducted no interviews or surveys of our own: the interview evidence is secondary, drawn from five studies we selected through targeted searches, and the App Store reviews were checked only by an AI-assisted second pass (82.5% agreement). Peer values are published aggregates without sampling errors. All financial inputs are unvalidated assumptions with no churn, tax or financing flows.
[Nor] Future scope. Priority extensions are (i) consent-based interviews with merchants, first-time users and institutional budget owners to test willingness to pay; (ii) design-based estimation if strata and cluster identifiers become available; (iii) pooling Findex rounds [8, 9, 15] and extending the comparison from published aggregates to country-level microdata for peer economies; (iv) an evaluated pilot with pre-specified outcomes and a power calculation before treating verified loss as an outcome [32]; and (v) extending the venture model with churn, taxes, receivable timing and month-by-month cash flow.
[Hea] 6. Conclusion
[Nor] This paper asked whether the spread of mobile banking in India comes with higher cybersecurity risk, and for whom, and what that means for an entrepreneurial response. Using the 2024 India Global Findex, it found that account ownership is widespread (89.0%) but digital payment use (48.5%) and device protection (57.7% of phone owners) are lower and unevenly distributed by gender, age and income. Payment users reported more unsolicited money requests than non-users (13.1% versus 4.3%), and the association remained after adjustment (odds ratio 3.45). Across demographic groups, exposure rose only weakly with inclusion, was also high among the poorest, and protection lagged inclusion at older ages, so risk concentrates where inclusion outruns safeguards rather than rising evenly with it. The literature shows that benefits of digital finance depend on use and trust, and that scams exploit ordinary behaviour; the results are consistent with both ideas, although they do not prove causation.
[Nor] For entrepreneurs and institutions, the evidence supports testing an accessible, institution-funded payment-safety service in a staged pilot. An illustrative model reaches steady-state break-even at eight paying institutions and a three-year surplus in the base case, but the result is highly sensitive to price, support cost, acquisition cost and renewal, and it is not evidence of demand. App reviews of payment and bank apps on both major platforms added that user grievances concern rewards, refunds, account blocks, charges, support and security prompts far more than third-party fraud, while five published interview studies describe the social engineering, trust and slow redress that reviews rarely capture, and a peer comparison placed India near the top on account ownership but below peers on phone ownership. The contribution of the study is therefore a transparent description of where access and safe use diverge, and a validated-assumption-first path from that pattern to a testable venture. Pilot evidence on willingness to pay, retention and effectiveness is the necessary next step.
