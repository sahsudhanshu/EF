                  Mobile Banking: Financial Inclusion vs Cybersecurity Risks

 Evidence from India's 2024 Global Findex, public app reviews and published interview studies,
                                     with implications for entrepreneurs

     Rushal Verma (IIT2024045), Vichanshu Raj (IIT2024083) and Sudhanshu (IIT2024081)
                         Group 31, Entrepreneurial Finance | 20 November 2026

Abstract

This study asks whether the spread of mobile banking and digital payments in India comes with
higher cybersecurity risk, for whom, and what this implies for entrepreneurs serving first-time
digital users. We analyse the 2024 India Global Findex survey of 3,000 adults with survey
weights and an exploratory logistic model, compare India with peer economies, code 14,088
public reviews of 13 payment and bank apps from two app stores, and synthesise five published
interview studies. Account ownership was 89.0%, but only 48.5% made or received a digital
payment and 57.7% of phone owners reported a device lock. Payment users reported more
unsolicited money requests than non-users (13.1% against 4.3%; adjusted odds ratio 3.45), yet
exposure was also high in the lowest income quintile and protection lagged inclusion at older
ages. Reviews rarely described theft; complaints concerned rewards, refunds, account blocks and
security prompts. An illustrative model for an institution-funded safety service breaks even at
eight paying institutions. The data cannot show causation, and the commercial assumptions need
pilot validation.

Keywords: financial inclusion; mobile banking; digital payments; cybersecurity; fraud exposure;
Global Findex; entrepreneurial finance; India. JEL codes: G21, G23, L26, O16.

1. Introduction

Access to formal finance is linked with household resilience, enterprise formation and poverty
reduction [1, 2], and mobile phones and digital payments have changed how it is delivered [3, 4],
in India with public digital identity and payment infrastructure [5, 6]. For a small merchant, a
first-time business owner or any digital customer, an account helps only if it can be used reliably
and without undue risk. Financial inclusion here means use of formal financial services: account
ownership measures access, digital payment use measures activity. Reported cybersecurity
exposure means a report of an unsolicited call or message asking for money, a sign of a possible
scam attempt and not verified fraud. The World Bank's Global Findex is the main cross-country
source for the first two measures [7, 8, 9].

Problem and gap. Mobile banking can bring people into the formal system, but each new user is
also a possible target and may lack the safeguards of experienced users; deception exploits
ordinary behaviour [10, 11], and trust shapes adoption [12, 13]. The literature is strong on
mobile-money benefits and on phishing, but rarely joins them in a recent national survey of
India, and little work turns such patterns into an entrepreneurial question: what service could be
built, who would pay, and what would make it viable. We ask where inclusion and cybersecurity
risk move together, where they do not, and what institutions and entrepreneurs could do.

Research questions. RQ1: How do account ownership, digital payment use and device
protection differ by gender, age and income, and how does India compare with peer economies?
RQ2: Are payment users more likely than non-users to report unsolicited money requests, after
adjustment? RQ3: Among people who report a request, is a device lock associated with fewer
reporting that they sent money? RQ4: Which groups show the largest gap between digital
payment use and device protection? RQ5: What do public app reviews, institutional documents
and published interview studies reveal about fraud grievances, security friction and support?

2. Literature review

This review covers inclusion and mobile money, adoption and trust, security behaviour and
fraud, and cyber-risk economics (35 academic sources), and ends with the gap this study fills.

2.1 Inclusion, mobile money and development

Deeper finance disproportionately raises the incomes of the poorest [14], and account access
supports inclusive growth depending on how accounts are used [1]. Measurement advanced with
the Global Findex [7] and its rounds [8, 9], which separated ownership from usage. The best
causal evidence is from Kenya: M-PESA helped households share risk [15] and lifted around 2%
of households out of poverty, more in female-headed households [2]. Mobile transfers improved
diets in Niger [16], debit cards raised savings in Mexico [17], diffusion varies by institutional
context [18], and digital inclusion is linked with growth [19]. In India, biometric payments
reduced leakage [5] and demonetisation was used to study digital payments [6]; gains are not
automatic, as microcredit raised business investment but not average consumption [20]. Benefits
arise when services are used, so this study measures use, not ownership alone.

2.2 Entrepreneurial finance, adoption, trust and fintech

Entrepreneurial finance starts from information asymmetry: lenders ration credit even in
equilibrium because higher rates worsen the mix of borrowers [21]; small and young firms rely
on different sources as they grow and become more transparent [22]; access to finance constrains
small-firm growth [23]; and binding liquidity constraints keep would-be entrepreneurs out of
business [24]. Digital data can ease this constraint, since simple digital-footprint variables match
or exceed credit-bureau scores in predicting default, including for unscorable customers [25].
Inclusion therefore matters for entrepreneurship, but the same digital channels expose small
merchants to the fraud described below. Adoption depends on perceived usefulness and ease of
use [26]; for mobile banking, credibility, self-efficacy and cost shape intention [12]. Fintech
changes industry structure and user expectations [4] and is a driver of inclusion that needs digital
identity and interoperable payments [27], but digital finance has persistent unresolved issues
[13]. Safety and trust are conditions for continued use, not extras.

2.3 Security behaviour, phishing and fraud

Users reject protections that conflict with their tasks [28]. Well-designed fake websites deceive
many participants [10]; awareness of phishing risk is unrelated to perceived vulnerability, so
people manage familiar risks but not unfamiliar ones [29]; and fraud victims are diverse, with
scams exploiting authority, small sums, pressure and trust [11, 30]. Awareness campaigns often
fail to change behaviour [31], though short online education reduced fraud susceptibility in a
U.S. experiment, lasting three months only with a reminder [32].

2.4 Cyber risk, incentives and the research gap

Security is under-provided because incentives and externalities separate those who bear losses
from those who can prevent them [33]; optimal spending depends on vulnerability and potential
loss [34]; and cyber risk is a market failure and source of instability [35]. Three conclusions
follow: inclusion benefits depend on use [1, 2, 15]; adoption depends on trust while security
failures exploit ordinary behaviour [10, 11, 12]; and safety is a shared responsibility that markets
under-supply [33, 35]. Missing is a recent national view for India that places adoption, device

protection and reported scam exposure side by side, adds what users say in their own words, and
links the pattern to a venture and its viability. This study addresses that gap.

3. Methodology

3.1 Research design

The design is cross-sectional, observational and descriptive-analytical: weighted descriptives and
group comparisons (RQ1, RQ4); a weighted logistic model (RQ2) and lock-status comparison
(RQ3); a peer comparison; qualitative analysis of documents, app reviews and published
interview studies (RQ5); and a deterministic financial model (RQ6). Justification: a population
survey is the only source recording adoption and exposure for the same adults; interviews of our
own were not feasible, so published studies serve as secondary evidence; and scenario modelling
is the standard test of a venture before commercial data exist.

3.2 Sampling method and participants

We use the India file of the Global Findex Database 2025 (survey year 2024,
IND_2024_FINDEX_v02_M) [36]: 3,000 respondents aged 15 or older, a nationally
representative adult sample drawn by the World Bank and Gallup. All percentages use the survey
weight (wgt); n and counts are unweighted. The data describe individuals, not businesses.
Justification: it is the standard public inclusion benchmark and the only source linking
payments to phone-safety items in India.

3.3 Data collection methods

Seven public sources were used: the India Findex microdata [36]; Findex 2025 country
indicators for India and nine peers [37]; Google Play reviews of five payment apps (PhonePe,
Google Pay, Paytm, BHIM, SBI YONO; 5,758 reviews, 2 Oct to 19 Nov 2026) [38]; App Store
reviews of the same apps (2,090; 30 Apr to 18 Nov) [39]; reviews of eight bank apps in both
stores (6,240; 5 Jun to 19 Nov) [40]; three institutional documents [41, 42, 43]; and five
published interview studies [44, 45, 46, 47, 48] found by targeted search on 20 November 2026
(not a systematic review). Reviews were collected with google-play-scraper and Apple's public
feed; only app, date, rating and text were kept (no names, emails or phone numbers), and reviews
under 15 characters and duplicates were removed. We conducted no interviews or surveys of our
own. Justification: public data permit reproduction, reviews give unprompted user language,
and published studies supply victim and merchant experience that reviews rarely contain.

3.4 Materials and instruments

Python (pandas, numpy, matplotlib) was used; scripts, an audit file and the coding scheme are in
the submission folder. Variables: account, anydigpayment (made or received a digital payment in
the past year), con1 (phone), con18 (lock; 2,141 valid), con21 (unsolicited request; 2,145 phone
owners) and con22 (sent money; 219 recipients).

3.5 Data analysis

Survey. A weighted share is the sum of weights of positive responses over the sum of weights of
valid responses, with each subgroup using its own denominator. For RQ2 the outcome is a
reported request among phone owners, with payment use, gender, age, education, income
quintile, workforce status and urban residence as predictors, estimated by weighted maximum
likelihood with an independent-observation sandwich covariance; the public file has no strata or
cluster identifiers, so intervals are approximate, and a Kish design effect and assumed extra
effects of 1.5, 2 and 3 serve as sensitivity checks. For RQ4 each group's payment-use share (all

                                                          3
adults) is set beside its lock share (phone owners) and, for the central question, its exposure
across 18 groups with Spearman rank correlations; denominators differ, so this is descriptive.
RQ3 uses a Fisher exact test on unweighted counts, and peer values are compared without tests.

Qualitative. Document passages on trust, shared responsibility and credential protection were
grouped into themes. Reviews were coded in two steps: dictionaries flagged five themes (fraud,
failed payment, support, account blocking or verification, safety language) plus security-friction
prompts for bank apps, then every fraud-keyword review was read and placed in one of seven
categories by one coder. Blind AI second passes agreed on 112 of 118 Google Play reviews
(94.9%, kappa 0.93), 47 of 57 App Store reviews (82.5%, 0.78) and 57 of 65 bank-app reviews
(87.7%, 0.84). A human coder outside the group, blind to the labels, re-coded 40 of the 118
Google Play reviews (38 agreed, 95.0%, kappa 0.93). A further second coder (a person assisted
by coding software, as reported by the group), who coded all 122 App Store and bank-app fraud-
keyword reviews without seeing the first-pass labels, agreed with the first pass on 102 (83.6%,
kappa 0.80; App Store 80.7%, 0.76; bank apps 86.2%, 0.81) and with the AI second pass on 104
(85.2%, 0.82). Bank and payment apps were compared by chi-square tests (Holm adjustment).
Published-study findings were mapped to eight themes from the full texts by one coder; counts
are of studies, not respondents.

Financial model. The model is deterministic and illustrative; no input comes from Findex. Base
assumptions are a fee of INR 50,000 per institution per month, variable cost INR 10,000,
acquisition cost INR 2 lakh and fixed cost INR 3 lakh a month (growing 20% a year), with fees
of INR 40,000 and 60,000 in the conservative and optimistic scenarios. Customers grow linearly
from zero with no churn; tax, interest and prelaunch spending are excluded. Amounts are in INR
lakh (1 lakh = INR 100,000).

3.6 Ethical considerations

De-identified public microdata are used under the World Bank's access terms and not
redistributed; reviews are public with no usernames stored; published studies are summarised,
not reproduced. Exposure is reported as reports, not verified fraud, and commercial figures are
illustrative with no partner, investor or willingness to pay claimed. Future interviews or pilots
would need informed consent and would not collect credentials.

4. Results

Percentages are survey-weighted unless stated; counts and n are unweighted. This section reports
findings without interpretation.

4.1 Adoption, exposure and device protection

Account ownership was 89.0% and digital payment use 48.5% (a 40.6-point difference); phone
ownership was 66.5%. Women's payment use was 42.7% and men's 54.1%. Of phone owners
with valid answers, 57.7% reported a device lock. Lock use was 50.3% for women and 62.7%
for men, 75.7% at ages 15 to 24 and 22.9% at 60 and above, and 40.6% in the lowest income
quintile and 75.2% in the highest. Among phone owners 9.4% reported an unsolicited request
(13.1% of payment users, 4.3% of non-users), and 21.0% of the 219 recipients reported sending
money (Table 1).

4.2 Adjusted association and lock status

In the adjusted model (2,145 phone owners; 219 positive) the odds ratio for payment users versus
non-users was 3.45 (approximate 95% interval 2.24 to 5.30) and for women versus men 0.47
(0.32 to 0.69); tertiary versus primary education or less was 1.70 (1.03 to 2.80), and age, income,
workforce and urban residence had intervals including 1.00 (Table 2). With variance inflated for
an assumed extra design effect of 3, the payment-user interval was 1.64 to 7.26 and the women
interval 0.24 to 0.92, but the tertiary interval included 1.00 from an extra effect of 1.5. For RQ3,
25.0% of recipients whose phone had a lock (39 of 170) and 8.9% of those without (6 of 49)
reported sending money; the Fisher exact test gave p = 0.11.

4.3 Inclusion, exposure and protection by group

Payment use exceeded lock use most at ages 60 and above (56.6% against 22.9%; +33.7 points)
and fell short of it most at ages 15 to 24 (43.5% against 75.7%; -32.1). Across 18 overlapping
groups the rank correlation was 0.38 between payment use and exposure and 0.63 between
payment use and lock use (Figure 1). Exposure was highest among tertiary-educated adults
(17.9%, payment use 74.5%), the lowest income quintile (12.5%, payment use 34.8%) and men
(11.8%), and lowest at ages 60 and above (4.1%) and among women (5.7%).

4.5 Public app reviews

The datasets hold 5,758 Google Play and 2,090 App Store reviews of five payment apps and
6,240 reviews of eight bank apps (mean ratings 2.90, 3.31 and 2.49). Fraud-keyword reviews
were 2.0%, 2.7% and 1.0% of reviews (mean ratings 1.36, 1.51 and 1.22); support, account-
blocking and safety-language themes were each 3% to 8% of reviews in every dataset, and failed
payments 0.4% to 2.3%. After every fraud-keyword review was read, 50.8% of Google Play
payment-app reviews concerned rewards or cashback not honoured, against 29.8% on the App
Store and 4.6% for bank apps, while 38.5% of bank-app reviews concerned blocked accounts,
mis-selling, charges or poor grievance handling (Figure 2). Reviews of unauthorised access or
theft numbered 6 (0.1%), 2 (0.1%) and 3 (below 0.1%). Security-friction prompts matched 2.4%
of bank-app reviews (mean rating 1.59) and 3.9% of payment-app reviews (2.09); 104 Google
Play payment-app reviews (1.8%) described device-lock prompts, mostly for BHIM. Bank and
payment apps differed after Holm adjustment (p < 0.001) for fraud keywords, failed payments,
safety language and security friction, but not for support (p = 0.87) or account blocking (p =
0.12). The second coder labelled 8 of the 122 App Store and bank-app reviews as unauthorised
access or theft (first pass 5; about 0.1% of 8,330 reviews); 10 of its 20 disagreements with the
first pass were reviews the first pass had labelled general accusations. A Hindi and Hinglish

4.6 Institutional documents and published studies
The documents yield three themes: trust linked with risk awareness and timely dispute resolution
[41]; consumer protection alongside financial capability [42]; and RBI calls for awareness
campaigns and protection of credentials such as OTPs and PINs [43]. Of eight themes in the five
published studies, social engineering, trust in the counterparty or bank, and low awareness
appeared in all five; weak support or redress and reliance on family or informal channels in four;
differences by gender, age or vulnerability in three; and protective advice and design cues and
merchant harms in two each. Examples: 45 of 46 participants said trust in the receiver matters
[45]; 12 of 24 merchants reported fake payment confirmations [46]; and of 91 victims who
reported fraud, 59 could not recover their money [48].
4.7 Illustrative financial model outputs
Contribution per institution is INR 40,000 a month (80%) and simple acquisition payback is 5
months. Steady-state break-even is 8 paying institutions, or 13 when one new institution is
acquired each month. The base case records a deficit of INR 32.80 lakh in Year 1, zero in Year 2
and a surplus of INR 52.16 lakh in Year 3; the conservative case is in deficit throughout (INR
36.80, 30.00 and 17.44 lakh) and the optimistic case reaches INR 48.80 and 173.16 lakh in Years
2 and 3. Cutting the fee to INR 40,000 raises break-even to 10 institutions, raising variable cost
to INR 15,000 raises it to 9, and an acquisition cost of INR 3 lakh extends payback to 7.5
months; across all fee and cost combinations tested the requirement is 6 to 12.
5. Discussion
Inclusion versus cybersecurity risk. Payment users reported more unsolicited requests than
non-users and the association survived adjustment, so inclusion in digital payments goes with
greater reachability by scammers, plausibly because digital users are reachable through more
channels [30]. Across groups the link was weak to moderate (rank correlation 0.38): exposure
was highest among the most included (tertiary-educated) but also in the lowest income quintile,
and lowest among older adults and women. Protection rose with inclusion (0.63) except among
older adults, whose payment use far exceeds lock use. Risk therefore concentrates where
inclusion outruns safeguards and where people have little margin for loss. A lock limits access to

                                                          7
a handset, not deception [10, 11, 28]; the RQ3 comparison rests on 45 senders and is not
significant.

Reviews, studies and documents. Review complaints concern rewards, refunds, support, blocks
and security prompts far more than theft, on both platforms. This does not measure scam
prevalence: victims may not write reviews, and the interview studies describe the social
engineering, trust and slow redress that reviews rarely capture; support and redress failures
appear in both sources, consistent with the documents [41, 42, 43]. In bank apps the tension
shows up as friction and weak redress more than fraud: SIM binding, biometric and root-
detection prompts were a recurring source of one-star reviews. Against peers, India has near-top
account ownership but below-peer phone ownership and digital payment use and the lowest
reported request share among economies with a value; recall, reporting and phone-based contact
may explain this, and the comparison sets a sample estimate against published values without
sampling error. Table 4 summarises the answers to the research questions.

5.1 Academic implications

The study joins the economics of inclusion [1, 2, 15] with the behavioural study of scams [10,
11, 31] and with entrepreneurial finance [21, 23], pairing national statistics with safety indicators
and user-generated text to show where access and safe use diverge. It shows how to label
intervals when design variables are missing and suggests that technology-acceptance models [12,
26] include perceived safety and device safeguards.

5.2 Policy implications

Each implication is a hypothesis to test. Inclusion targets should track active and safe use, not
only ownership [41, 42]. Awareness efforts such as the RBI's [43] should be judged on behaviour
change and designed for older, lower-income and women users, since generic campaigns often
fail and education persisted in one experiment only with reminders [31, 32]. Providers should
own warnings, refunds and complaint handling, regulators clear reporting channels, and users
credential hygiene [35]; security prompts should be tested so they do not lock out users.

                                               8
5.3 Managerial implications

The results suggest a narrow pilot of a multilingual payment-safety companion distributed
through banks, payment providers or merchant networks, paid for by the institution and free to
users: short audio and visual explanations, a pre-payment checklist and verified routes to support
and official reporting. It would not hold money, guarantee recovery or ask for an OTP or PIN. In
canvas terms the institution is the customer, revenue is a recurring subscription and costs are a
small fixed team, delivery and acquisition. Campaigns [43], in-app warnings, the national
helpline and in-house builds already cover parts of the problem, so the service must add
measurable value through local-language journeys with comprehension checks and faster routes
to verified support. Proposed plan: months 1-2 discovery with one sponsor, months 3-5 a
controlled pilot with prespecified outcomes, scale from month 6 only after a paid commitment.
No pilot has been run.

Financial interpretation. Eight institutions cover fixed costs only in steady state; about 13 are
needed while growing. A 20% lower fee adds two institutions, and the base case has no cushion
in Year 2, so churn or slow sales would produce a deficit; the conservative case does not break
even in three years. An illustrative INR 75 lakh seed (staged equity or grant) would cover the
Year 1 deficit (32.80), prelaunch build (10.00) and pilot evaluation (5.00), a working-capital
reserve (12.00) and contingency (15.20), but in the conservative case deficits plus prelaunch
costs reach INR 81.80 lakh by Year 2 and exceed it. Main risks are weak willingness to pay,
support cost above the margin and dependence on one partner; these conclusions follow security-
investment economics [33, 34] and hold only if prices and costs are validated with real buyers.

5.4 Future scope and limitations

Limitations. The survey is cross-sectional and self-reported; requests are not verified fraud, cells
such as the 45 senders are small, intervals are approximate (the tertiary-education result does not
survive assumed clustering), and the data describe individuals, not enterprises. Reviews are self-
selected, cover different windows, and were keyword-coded by one coder with AI second passes
and second-coder checks on samples; a Hindi and Hinglish extension changed no conclusion. We
conducted no interviews or surveys of our own, and the five published studies were selected by
us and are small. Peer values are aggregates without sampling errors, and all financial inputs are
unvalidated assumptions without churn, tax or financing flows.

Future scope. Priorities are consent-based interviews to test willingness to pay, design-based
estimation, peer-country microdata, an evaluated and powered pilot [32], and a venture model
with churn and cash flow.

6. Conclusion

This paper asked whether the spread of mobile banking in India comes with higher cybersecurity
risk, and for whom. Account ownership is widespread (89.0%), but digital payment use (48.5%)
and device protection (57.7%) are lower and unevenly distributed by gender, age and income.
Payment users reported more unsolicited requests than non-users (13.1% against 4.3%; odds
ratio 3.45), yet across groups exposure rose only weakly with inclusion, was high among the
poorest, and protection lagged inclusion at older ages: risk concentrates where inclusion outruns
safeguards. App reviews on both platforms and five published studies show that grievances
concern rewards, refunds, account blocks, support and security prompts far more than reported
theft, while social engineering and slow redress dominate the interview evidence. For
entrepreneurs and institutions the evidence supports a staged pilot of an accessible, institution-
funded safety service; the illustrative model breaks even at eight paying institutions but is highly

                                                          9
sensitive to price, support and acquisition costs and is not evidence of demand. Pilot evidence on
willingness to pay and effectiveness is the next step.
